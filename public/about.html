why this exists

Forecasts give numbers.
People need to know what to do.

Prakira is a small tool that turns the next three days of heat, rain, air quality and UV into a few actions for one household, and shows the numbers behind every action. This page explains why it was made and which research shaped each choice.

The gap we kept running into

Open weather and air-quality data is now free and very good. A phone can tell you that the heat index will reach 37 °C and the air quality index 204. What it does not tell you is what that means for a grandmother, a toddler, or someone who works on a roof all day.

Language models can write that kind of advice in seconds. They can also state a number that was never in the forecast, in the same confident tone. For a tool about safety, that is the wrong trade.

So the question behind Prakira was narrow: can an AI write the advice while fixed rules and a checker keep every number honest?

Six findings that shaped the design

While building it we searched the research literature (36 Scopus queries, 38 references kept). Each card below is one finding and what Prakira does because of it. We read the abstracts of these papers, not always the full text.

Ou and colleagues, 2025 · Water

Standard warnings miss the question people ask

Public warning systems send the same message to everyone. People want to know how it affects them now and what they can do about it.

So Prakiraasks who lives in the home and writes one action per serious hazard for that household.
doi.org/10.3390/w17071055
MacKay and colleagues, 2026 · Journal of Health Communication

AI heat messages were accurate but hard to read

Of 31 heat-health messages written by ChatGPT-4o, most were accurate, none met a grade 8 reading level, and few placed heat in the context of climate change.

So Prakirameasures reading level in its evaluation, and compares each day with the local 30-year normal.
doi.org/10.1080/10810730.2026.2709807
Kox and colleagues, 2025 · International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction

Experts want tailored, multilingual warnings, with accountability

Weather-warning experts saw tailored and multilingual warnings as the promising use of AI. Their concerns were over-reliance, less human oversight, and losing a single authoritative voice.

So Prakiraworks in two languages, shows what it rejected, and points to the national meteorological service instead of replacing it.
doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2025.105817
Karimanzira (first author), 2025 · Algorithms

Hallucination is a named risk in hazard communication

Work on language models for flood communication names hallucination as a key risk, and reports that grounding the model in data cut factual inconsistency by more than 75%.

So Prakiragoes one step further for numbers: a claim is shown only if every number matches the forecast value it cites.
doi.org/10.3390/a18110713
LeClerc and Joslyn, 2015 · Risk Analysis

False alarms and false certainty both hurt

In a weather-decision experiment, very high and very low false-alarm rates led to worse decisions. Showing the uncertainty of the forecast improved both compliance and decision quality.

So Prakirastays quiet when nothing is serious for the household, and shows the chance of rain beside the amount.
doi.org/10.1111/risa.12336
Chandra and colleagues, 2025 · Current Environmental Health Reports

Heat warnings should use more than temperature

A review of heat-health warning systems found most rely on temperature alone, and recommends humidity-aware heat-stress indices, personal factors, and digital tools for targeted warnings.

So Prakiracomputes the US National Weather Service heat index from temperature and humidity, and lets the household change which level counts as serious.
doi.org/10.1007/s40572-025-00496-5

What Prakira is not

Being clear about limits is part of the design.

  • Not the first of its kind. ClimApp and the HEAT-SHIELD platform already give personalised heat warnings from forecasts, with stronger physiology and no language model. Language models already write flood and air-quality advisories in research prototypes.
  • Not an official warning. It is a companion to your national meteorological service.
  • Not medical advice. No clinician has reviewed it.
  • Not a sensor. The data are model forecasts for a grid cell several kilometres wide.
  • Not fully verified. Numbers and levels are checked. The wording of each action is written by a language model and is not.

What it does add

  • Heat, rain, air and UV in one briefing for one household, with a flag when strong heat and polluted air fall on the same day.
  • A checker that removes any AI claim whose number or level does not match the data, and a list of what was removed.
  • Fixed notices for any serious hazard the AI did not cover.
  • A public evaluation with its weak points stated: pass rate, coverage, reading level, and locations the system was never tuned on.

We did not find this combination in our literature search. That is not proof that it is new: we searched one database and did not search preprints.

Who made it, and how

Prakira was built in October 2026 for ForgeHacks 2026 (track: AI + Climate) by Rofi, a student in Indonesia, with AI coding assistance. The name comes from the Indonesian word prakiraan, forecast.

It runs on open data from Open-Meteo and the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, and a small open-weight language model from Mistral. It has no dependencies beyond Node.js, and the whole method is public.

Source code and method

The theory behind the layout of each item

The Protective Action Decision Model (Lindell and Perry, 2012) describes how people respond to warnings: they must receive, notice and understand the message, then judge the threat, the action, and the source before they act.

Each briefing item follows that order: what is forecast, for whom it matters, one thing to do, and where the number came from.
